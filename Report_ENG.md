# Ethical Assessment of Russian Presidential Decrees No. 71 and No. 72 of 21 February 2022

Report under HAI-LEAF and the Multi-Agent Protocol  
Assessment date: 29 October 2026

## 1. Executive Assessment

The combined mechanism established by the decrees is ethically unacceptable under the criteria of sovereign equality, human rights and safety, evidential integrity, and responsibility for military decisions.

The critical elements are the unilateral recognition of territorial entities within Ukraine and the military directive justified by requests from their leaders. The stated grounds—popular will, Ukraine’s alleged refusal of peaceful settlement, and the need for “peacekeeping”—are not substantiated within the acts themselves.

This conclusion does not rest solely on missing evidence in short documents. External verification identifies a conflict with the agreed settlement framework and an explicit international assessment that the decision violated Ukraine’s sovereignty.

On 21 February 2022, the UN Secretary-General characterized Russia’s decision as a violation of Ukraine’s territorial integrity and sovereignty. On 2 March, the UN General Assembly, in resolution ES-11/1, paragraphs 5–6, deplored that decision and demanded its reversal. These are not judicial judgments; the ethical conclusion below also rests on its own reasoning. [2, 4]

Individual technical and diplomatic instructions are not treated as independent critical violations. Confidence is high regarding the decrees’ wording and the principal assessment. Their specific quantitative contribution to subsequent losses was not determined.

**A stated peaceful purpose does not establish an acceptable military means.**

## 4. Verification of the Stated Grounds

### Expression of Popular Will

The decrees do not specify:

- Who is included in “the people.”
- How their will was established.
- Whether conditions were free.
- How dissenters and people who had left were represented.
- Who independently verified the result.

This does not prove that no supporting material exists elsewhere. The appropriate evidential status is “not substantiated by the supplied acts.”

Even an established preference among part of the population does not automatically justify a particular form of external recognition or military action. Representation, other people’s rights, and the permissibility of the means require separate examination.

### Ukraine’s Alleged Refusal and the Minsk Agreements

The texts identify no specific Ukrainian decision, date of refusal, or evidence-based assessment of implementation by all parties. This report does not establish the full allocation of responsibility for implementing the Minsk agreements.

Security Council resolution 2202 of 17 February 2015 nevertheless reaffirmed Ukraine’s sovereignty and territorial integrity and endorsed the Package of Measures. [6]

On 21 February 2022, the OSCE Special Representative in the Trilateral Contact Group explicitly identified a contradiction between recognition and the Minsk agreements, including their objective that the relevant areas remain part of Ukraine with a special status. [3]

Invoking the Minsk agreements therefore does not justify a decision that changes their underlying territorial framework.

### “Peacekeeping”

Paragraph 4 names a function but does not establish its actual peacekeeping character. The decrees are not a UN mandate.

On 22 February 2022, the UN Secretary-General rejected the characterization of troops entering another state’s territory without its consent as peacekeepers. [5]

The absence of a UN mandate alone does not establish that every international mission is unlawful. Here, the decisive questions concern the specific basis for intervention, the consent of the state whose territory is affected, necessity, limits on force, and accountability.

## 5. TPM Reconstruction

| Component | Textual basis | Reconstruction and status |
|---|---|---|
| P — Perception | Paragraphs 1 and 4 | The situation is framed through popular will, blocked settlement, and a need for an external response. Wording explicit; framing function reconstructed |
| I — Information | Popular will, Ukraine’s alleged refusal, leaders’ requests | Claims present; sources, representativeness, and verification not established by the texts |
| J — Judgment | Transition from premises to instructions | These circumstances are presented as sufficient for recognition and a military decision. Justification partly reconstructed |
| D — Decision | Paragraphs 1–5 | Recognition, diplomatic negotiations, treaty preparation, a military function, and immediate effect are explicit |

Verification of information and normative justification are separate tasks.

Even an authentic request from an entity’s leader does not establish that the leader represents all residents or that the request provides sufficient grounds for external military intervention.

The central defect is the inadequately justified transition from asserted circumstances to the chosen means.

TPM reconstructs the documents’ logic. It does not establish the author’s psychological processes or hidden motives.

**An act cannot establish the truth of its own premises.**

## 6. Six Ethical Perspectives

| Perspective | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Ethical egoism — P > D | There is a risk that the issuing state’s position substitutes for the interests of all affected people. Hidden motives are not established by the decrees |
| Deontology — P > J > D | Protecting people does not remove requirements governing permissible means. A local leader’s request is not a sufficient justification for intervention |
| Utilitarianism — I > J > D | No comparison is provided between military action, negotiations, international monitoring, and other alternatives |
| Relativism — I > P > D | Conflict context helps explain decisions but does not convert unilateral assertions into verified facts |
| Virtue ethics — P > I > J > D | Relevant considerations include honesty in justification, restraint, and responsibility for foreseeable harm |
| Ethics of care — I > P > J > D | An abstract “people” does not capture the diversity of individuals, needs, and political positions |

Particular attention is required for civilians on both sides of the contact line, children, older people, people with disabilities, dissenting residents, and displaced persons.

Residence in a territory does not imply agreement with its leader.

The six perspectives are not averaged into a moral score. Administrative convenience or benefits to one party do not compensate for critical risks imposed on others.

## 7. Feedback and Consequences

The decrees establish no independent procedure for checking their central assertions or revising the initial framing in response to contrary evidence.

A risk of self-confirmation can be reconstructed:

> Recognition of an entity > acceptance of its leader’s request > a military directive > reinforcement of the original political framing.

This is an analytical risk model, not a demonstrated account of every actual procedure.

| Evidential level | Finding |
|---|---|
| Directly established by the text | A change in Russia’s position; diplomatic, treaty-related, and military instructions |
| Reasonably foreseeable risk | Escalation, danger to civilians, displacement, and narrowing of the negotiating space |
| Externally corroborated assessment | International condemnation of the recognition decision |
| Not established by this study | Specific losses and the quantitative causal contribution of each decree |

The OSCE representative had already warned against further military action and bloodshed on 21 February. [3]

The subsequent resolution ES-11/1 deplored Russia’s aggression and demanded withdrawal of its forces. It supports the wider historical context but does not replace an investigation into each decree’s causal contribution. [4]

## 8. Critical Review and Alternative Interpretation

The strongest good-faith alternative interpretation is that protecting civilians and taking account of self-determination could be legitimate aims.

Justifying the specific measures would nevertheless require verified representation, a proper legal basis, necessity, proportionality, less dangerous alternatives, and independent oversight.

**An acceptable abstract purpose does not establish an acceptable decision.**

The second review round confirmed the following limits:

- These are not decrees incorporating the territories into Russia.
- They contain no explicit order to launch an offensive across all of Ukraine.
- They do not establish precise territorial boundaries.
- “Until the treaty is concluded” is not a calendar deadline or an explicit withdrawal requirement.
- Paragraphs 2–3 do not establish completed negotiations or concluded treaties.
- Missing safeguards in these decrees do not prove their absence from every related document.

Uncertainty remains regarding primary evidence of popular will, the full contents of the requests, and the complete body of military instructions.

These uncertainties limit detail but do not remove the principal assessment in the verified context.

## 9. Practical Implications

Replacing the phrase “peacekeeping” or adding technical safeguards would be insufficient. The underlying decision on unilateral alteration of territorial status and the military mechanism requires reconsideration.

For comparable acts, HAI-LEAF should require separate verification of:

1. The representativeness and freedom of the claimed expression of popular will.
2. The authority of the person requesting external intervention.
3. The permissibility of military means.
4. Necessity and less dangerous alternatives.
5. Protection of all affected people.
6. Termination conditions, oversight, and correction.

Confidence is high regarding the textual mechanisms and principal assessment. Confidence in the reconstructed self-confirmation loop is medium.

The potential severity of harm associated with the military component is critical. Severity and confidence are distinct dimensions.
